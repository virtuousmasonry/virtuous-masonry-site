What to ask before hiring a mason in the Lehigh Valley

Specific questions, real numbers to check, and the red flags that mean it's time to call someone else.

Why this list is worth ten minutes

Bad masonry work is expensive to fix twice — once for the original job, again to redo it correctly. And in a freeze-thaw climate, a mistake that looks fine in October can be visibly failing by March. Most of what separates a contractor who gets it right from one who doesn't comes down to things you can actually check before signing anything, not luck.

Licensing and insurance — what to actually verify

Ask for the contractor's Pennsylvania Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration number and look it up yourself rather than taking their word for it. Then ask for proof of insurance directly — general liability and workers' compensation, not just a claim that they "have insurance." For residential masonry work, liability coverage in the range of $1–2 million is a reasonable minimum to ask about. If a contractor hesitates to produce either document, that's the answer.

Permits and local codes

Certain masonry projects in Allentown, Bethlehem, and Easton require permits — retaining walls above a certain height and structural chimney work are common examples. A contractor who suggests skipping the permit to save time or money is putting the liability on you, not them, if something goes wrong or the work needs to be undone for a future sale.

Material matching, especially on older homes

A lot of Lehigh Valley housing predates modern construction and was built with softer, lime-based mortar. Ask directly what mortar mix they plan to use and why. A contractor who can't explain the difference between matching an older home's original mortar and using a modern hard mix hasn't thought about it — and using the wrong mix is one of the most common ways a repointing or repair job creates new cracking instead of fixing the original problem.

Who's actually doing the work

Ask directly: will the person quoting the job be the one on site, or does the work get subcontracted out? Crews change the dynamic of accountability — if something's off mid-job, you want to know who to call and whether they can actually answer. A one-crew, start-to-finish setup with no subcontracting is worth asking about specifically.

Getting the estimate in writing

A fair estimate should follow an actual on-site visit, not a phone quote, and should spell out materials, scope, and a fixed total price in writing — not a vague per-day or per-row number that can move once work starts. Ask what happens if the crew finds something unexpected once they start (a rotted substrate, drainage issue, etc.) — a contractor with a clear answer to that question has done this enough times to have one.

Scheduling and season timing

Spring and fall are generally the easiest seasons for masonry work in this climate. Winter and summer jobs are possible but need specific handling — mortar curing in cold temperatures, or crews working around summer heat and storms. Ask how they plan to handle the timing of your specific project rather than accepting a generic "we work year-round."

Red flags to walk away from

  • Pressure to sign same-day, or a "price today only" pitch.
  • A large upfront deposit before any materials are ordered or work has started.
  • No written estimate, or one that's vague on materials and scope.
  • Reluctance to provide insurance proof or a license number you can verify.
  • No references, or only references they hand-picked with no way to actually reach them.

Key takeaways

  • Verify PA HIC registration and insurance directly — don't take a verbal claim as proof.
  • Ask what mortar mix they'll use, especially on older Lehigh Valley homes.
  • Confirm who's actually on site doing the work, not just who's quoting it.
  • Get a fixed price in writing after an on-site visit, not a phone estimate.
  • Same-day pressure and large upfront deposits are the two biggest red flags.

FAQ

How much liability insurance should a mason carry?

$1–2 million in general liability is a reasonable minimum to ask about for residential masonry work, along with active workers' compensation coverage.

Do I need a permit for a retaining wall or chimney repair?

Often, yes, depending on height and scope — a licensed local contractor should be able to tell you whether your specific project needs one.

Should I get multiple quotes?

Yes, and comparing them on scope and materials specified, not just the bottom-line number, is what actually tells you whether you're comparing like for like.
